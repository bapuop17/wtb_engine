Saudi Arabia: Saudi's Gen. Turki al-Maliki: Coalition Intercepts 2 Houthi Missiles

Published November 15, 2026 • Category: GEOPOLITICS • Region: Saudi Arabia • Watch Directly on YouTube

1. COLD OPEN

Saudi Arabia's coalition just shot down two Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh, and the world is watching because any hiccup in the kingdom's capital could reverberate through global oil markets. Wall Street traders are sweating, fearing that a strike on the Eastern Province's refineries would choke the flow of crude that underpins the S&P 500 energy index. Meanwhile, Washington's defense chiefs are scrambling to assess whether this signals a wider Iran-backed escalation that could pull the United States deeper into the Yemen quagmire.

2. VISUAL EVIDENCE

Now look at this picture: a Houthi drone buzzing low over Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, the same region that pumps more than ten million barrels a day for the global market. On one side, General Turki al-Maliki's coalition is deploying Patriot and THAAD batteries to create a shield; on the other, the Houthis, backed by Iranian missile tech, are testing the limits of that shield with ever-more sophisticated drones. The stakes for an American investor are crystal clear - a successful strike could spike Brent crude by over $10 per barrel in a single day.

3. HISTORICAL PARADOX

Saudi Arabia spends roughly $10 billion a year on air-defense systems, yet the Houthi militia can still launch a handful of missiles that force the kingdom to divert resources. Let's say out of every 100 missiles the coalition can intercept, five slip through and hit a refinery, each causing $2 billion in lost output and cleanup. That means the kingdom is effectively paying $200 million per missile that gets through - a cost that quickly eats into its oil-revenue surplus and forces a re-allocation of its sovereign wealth fund.

4. MAP CHOKEPOINT

If you look at this map, the Red Sea corridor links the Suez Canal to the Gulf of Aden, and any disruption in Saudi ports forces tankers to detour around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10,000 nautical miles. When A - the Houthi missile strike - hits a terminal in the Eastern Province, B - the Red Sea shipping volume - drops by roughly 12 %, pushing freight rates up by $150 per barrel. That domino effect ripples through European refineries, Asian petrochemical plants, and even the U.S. Midwest, where diesel prices are already fragile.

5. MONEY IN THE MISSILE WAR

Saudi Arabia's air-defense triumphs aren't just pyrotechnic show-downs; they're a ledger of dollars, drones, and oil. Each Houthi missile costs roughly $3 million to produce, yet the coalition's interception system - an $8 billion network of Patriot batteries, THAAD, and local SAMs - spends $1.2 billion annually to stay ready. Multiply that by the 12 missiles intercepted last month, and you see a $36 million hit to the Houthi budget, but a $14 million drain on Saudi defense spending. Meanwhile, every drone strike on the Eastern Province pushes Aramco's refinery output down by 2 %, translating to a $1.5 billion dip in global oil supply. The math shows a war that costs the kingdom more than the rebels.

6. RED LINES CLASH

Saudi-led coalition, led by Gen. Turki Al-Maliki, pushes a hardline stance: any Houthi missile or drone over Riyadh or the Eastern Province is a direct attack on sovereign territory, warranting full retaliatory force. The Houthis, backed by Iran, see their red line as protecting Yemen's sovereignty; they claim their strikes target only civilian infrastructure, not the king's palace. The clash boils down to a classic asymmetry: Saudi's conventional firepower versus Houthi's low-cost, high-frequency raids. Each side's rhetoric masks a deeper calculus - Saudi wants to deter further escalation, while the Houthis aim to force concessions from Riyadh on Yemen's political future.

7. THE SAUDI-LED COALITION

The Saudi-led coalition in Yemen said Friday it intercepted and destroyed a drone over the Mecca region in western Saudi Arabia, after earlier downing two Houthi ballistic missiles targeting Najran. Saudi-led coalition intercepts a missile as fighting rages in Yemen's al-Jawf.

8. CHOKEPOINTS AT STAKE

Every missile that misses Riyadh is a ripple across the Red Sea corridor. The Bab el-Mandeb strait, a 12-mile choke point between Yemen and Djibouti, sees a 5 % uptick in shipping delays when Houthi drones patrol the airspace. The Strait of Hormuz, the artery of 20 % of the world's oil, feels the pressure as Saudi air-defenses scramble to intercept threats, forcing vessels to reroute through the longer Suez Canal. In the same breath, the US Navy's Fifth Fleet adjusts its deployment, while China's Belt-and-Road Initiative pauses new port contracts in Yemen.
