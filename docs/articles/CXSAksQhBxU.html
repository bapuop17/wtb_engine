Yemen's Bab al-Mandab Falls: Houthis Seize Red Sea Chokepoint as Saudi Forces Retreat

Published November 16, 2026 • Category: GEOPOLITICS • Region: Yemen • Watch Directly on YouTube

1. COLD OPEN: STRAIT LOST

Sunday morning satellite feeds confirmed what Riyadh and Washington feared: Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces have abandoned the Bab al-Mandab Strait after a six-week counter-offensive, handing Iran-backed Houthi rebels total control of the Red Sea's southern choke point. The internationally recognized government - based in Aden since the Houthis seized Sanaa in 2014 - just watched its coastal gains evaporate in a matter of days. Mocha port, the historic coffee hub the government recaptured in June, fell without a fight as Houthi fighters rolled in from the north. Now Houthi artillery sits on the cliffs overlooking the 18-mile-wide strait where 4.8 million barrels of oil transit daily. Moving troops away from the strait to defend Aden itself. That's not a redeployment - that's a strategic concession.

2. THE RIVALRY & STAKES

Now look at this picture: on one side, you have the Houthis - Ansar Allah - an Iran-backed Zaydi Shia movement that's spent a decade turning Yemen's mountains into a missile factory, and on the other, the Presidential Leadership Council in Aden, backed by Saudi air power, UAE training, and billions in Western aid. Why should a trader in New York or a commuter in London care? Because 12% of global seaborne trade passes through this strait - oil, LNG, grain, container ships - and Houthi anti-ship missiles now sit on both shores. Three questions drive this case study: First, how did a six-week Saudi-backed offensive collapse so fast? Second, what does Houthi control of the Hanish Islands mean for the southern Red Sea corridor? Third, can the Aden government survive when its own capital is now the primary target?

3. HISTORICAL PARADOX

Here's the paradox nobody in Washington likes to say out loud: the internationally recognized government hasn't controlled its own capital, Sanaa, since September 2014 - that's twelve years of governing from a temporary capital, Aden, while the Houthis built a state-within-a-state in the north. Let's do the simple math: the Presidential Leadership Council commands maybe 60,000 troops on paper, but they're fractured - Giants Bridge brigades, Southern Transitional Council separatists, Tihama coastal forces - each with different paymasters in Riyadh and Abu Dhabi. They field a cohesive 200,000-strong force with unified command, Iranian supply lines, and a tax base covering 80% of Yemen's population. When the offensive launched six weeks ago, the government committed 15,000 troops to the coast. They retook Mocha, advanced toward the strait - then the Houthis counter-attacked from three axes, cut supply lines, and the government folded. Sounds simple, right? But here's the problem: the Houthis didn't just win territory. They won the psychological edge.

4. MAP: CHOKEPOINT ANATOMY

If you look at this map, the geometry is brutal. The Bab al-Mandab - 'Gate of Tears' in Arabic - is just 18 miles wide at its narrowest, split by Perim Island into two channels: the eastern Alexander's Strait, 2 miles wide, and the western Dact-el-Mayun, 16 miles but shallow. Whoever holds the Yemeni cliffs - the Jabal al-Tair volcanic ridge and the Mocha highlands - owns the firing solution for every ship below. The Houthis now hold both. They seized the Hanish Islands - Greater Hanish, Little Hanish, and Suyul - 40 miles southwest of Mocha, giving them a forward radar and missile base covering the entire southern Red Sea. When A strikes B here, shipping doesn't just slow - it reroutes around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 14 days and $1.2 million in fuel per voyage. Insurance premiums for Red Sea transits have already quadrupled since January. Brent crude jumped $4 on Monday's open. This isn't theoretical - it's your gas pump price.

5. DOMINO CHAIN REACTION

The domino chain is already falling. Taiz - Yemen's third-largest city, 600,000 people - has been under Houthi siege for years, but now it's nearly fully encircled as Houthi forces push south from the mountains and east from the coast. The last supply road - the 'Burhani corridor' - is under direct fire. WHO says 17 million Yemeni need food aid; Taiz alone has 200,000 on the brink of famine. Meanwhile, Houthi ballistic missiles and drones struck Riyadh Sunday - two missiles, three drones, intercepted but the message landed: the war isn't at the border anymore, it's in the Saudi capital. That forced the Saudi redeployment: pull brigades from the Bab al-Mandab front, rush them to Aden's northern approaches. But Aden's defenses are thin - the Giants Bridge hold the west, STC the east, and nobody trusts each other. If the Houthis take the strait, they can land troops behind Aden.

6. PHILOSOPHY CLASH

While the Saudi-led coalition wants to use maximum pressure - airstrikes, blockade, designated terrorist labels - to force Houthi capitulation, the Houthis argue that resistance is the only language the West understands. Tehran calls it 'forward defense': every dollar spent arming Ansar Allah costs Riyadh a hundred in Patriot interceptors, naval deployments, and lost oil revenue. Since 2023, Iran has shipped the Houthis 358 anti-ship cruise missiles, 120 coastal defense radars, and enough drone components for 5,000 Shahed-136 variants - all via dhows across the Arabian Sea and overland through Oman. Maybe $200 million annually. A chokehold on global energy, a dagger at Saudi Arabia's throat, and a veto on any Red Sea security architecture. Follow the money: the coalition has spent $100 billion since 2015. The Houthis control more territory today than they did in 2018. That's not a stalemate - that's a strategic defeat wearing a ceasefire's clothes.

7. FOLLOW THE MONEY

Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM - the big three container lines - have rerouted 90% of Asia-Europe traffic around Africa. That's $300 million extra per month in fuel and charter costs, passed to you in higher prices for everything from iPhones to coffee. War risk insurance for Red Sea transits hit 1.5% of hull value - up from 0.3% in December. Meanwhile, the Houthis tax every ship that still dares the strait - 'protection fees' paid via third-party brokers in Dubai and Muscat, estimated $50 million monthly. They're footing the bill for the Aden government's payroll - $200 million a month - while UAE pays the STC. Washington has spent $5.4 billion on Operation Prosperity Guardian since December. The math is asymmetric: the Houthis spend pennies to impose dollars of cost. That's not warfare - that's economic engineering.

8. THE CAT ON THE WALL

So it's game over for the coalition, right? Quiet, neutral, Sultan Haitham's Muscat has been the backchannel for every Houthi-Saudi talk since 2022. Last week, Omani envoys shuttled between Sanaa and Riyadh with a draft: Houthi withdrawal from the Hanish Islands and Bab al-Mandab cliffs in exchange for Saudi recognition of Houthi administration in Sanaa, salary payments for Houthi civil servants, and a timeline for foreign troop exit. The Houthis said 'we'll study it.' Translation: they're buying time to dig in. But here's the kicker - the UAE hates this deal. Abu Dhabi sees Houthi legitimacy as an existential threat to its Red Sea port ambitions in Assab and Berbera. So the coalition's two pillars - Riyadh and Abu Dhabi - are split. They want de-escalation before the election cycle. The cat on the wall isn't a mediator - it's a spoiler with its own agenda.

9. SCENARIO ONE: SIEGE

Three scenarios from here. Scenario one: Houthi siege of Aden. They don't need to storm the city - they just cut the water pipeline from Bir Ahmed, blockade the port, and rain drones on the presidential palace. The PLC fractures: STC declares southern independence, Giants Bridge cut a side deal, the prime minister flees to Riyadh. Shipping avoids Red Sea entirely. Humanitarian catastrophe in Taiz and Aden - 2 million displaced. This is the 'Saigon 1975' playbook, and the Houthis have the patience to execute it.

10. SCENARIOS TWO & THREE

Scenario two: frozen conflict. Oman's deal holds - Houthis keep Sanaa and the north, withdraw from Hanish and the strait cliffs, UN monitors deploy, Saudi pays salaries, UAE gets security guarantees for Bab al-Mandab. Shipping slowly returns. But the Houthis keep their missiles - just pointed elsewhere. Scenario three: regional war. Israel strikes Houthi missile sites in Hodeidah after a drone hits Eilat. Iran activates Hezbollah, Iraqi militias, Houthis simultaneously. Strait closes completely. Depends on whether Riyadh and Abu Dhabi can agree on a price for peace - and whether Tehran decides the Houthis are more useful as a deterrent or a distraction.
